This widely popular species has become the topic of many myths and legends throughout the world.
It is often said that this bird is an omen of good luck or the protection offered by the soul of a deceased loved one.
Another common topic regarding robins, however, are explanations of their orange breast.
These are some that I've found.

## Christ's helper at the Cross
One of the most popular tales ascribes the robin's breast's color to Christ's blood.
It's set during the crucifixion of Jesus and narrates that, when Christ asked to drink, a robin approached him and gave him some water it was holding in its beak.
When landing on Jesus, the bird would have been stained with his blood, and its orange breast would be a symbol of it for future generations{citation1}.

Although this is the most common version, there are other variants that alter the context in which the bird approached Christ.
### Pulling out a thorn
This variant tells that, instead of giving Jesus water to drink,
the robin instead tried to pull one of the thorns from his head and got stained in the process{citation2}.
### A song to soothe pain
Here, the robin's purpose would've been to make the pain a bit more bearable.
Thus, it would've landed on Christ's shoulder to sing to his ear and then got stained with his blood.


## Dyed the color of fire
Many other popular folk legends explain the robin's breast's color as a symbol of fire,
usually as a consequence of burns sustained while performing some heroic act.
### Basque legend
In this Basque legend, Etsai (the devil) built a house where he'd lure hikers and curious passers-by to then nail them to the floor to steal their souls without a chance of their escaping.
One night, Amalur (mother Earth) went to that house to rescue the captives.
She lit a fire to do it, but while she was pulling out the nails, Etsai threw his cloak over it to disorient her.
At that point, a little *txantxangorri* (robin in Basque) blew on the flames to show Amalur where she'd lit the fire.
For this act of bravery, the txantxangorri's breast would be stained with the color of the flames{citation3}.
### Warming up Baby Jesus in the manger
Instead of helping Christ on the cross, here, the robin would've played a role during his birth.
In this tale, Mary was with the newborn Jesus in the manger and the fire was burning out.
She asked the animals for help, but none of them did.
At this point, a small bird appeared, which assisted her by bringing twigs to the flames.
Even then, fire was struggling to catch, so the bird flapped its wings to stoke the flames, so much so that it burned its breast, leaving it with its characteristic orange coloring{citation4}.
### In Welsh folklore
A Welsh tale narrates that the robin took pity of the damned souls and decided to collect water in its beak
and go down to the fiery pits of Hell—in some versions, to quench the sinners' thirst; in others, to put out the eternal flames{citation5}.
